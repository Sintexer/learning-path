
Large enterprises operate dozens of autonomous [[OLTP]] systems that require high availability and low latency for mission-critical business transactions. Because resource-heavy, ad-hoc analytical queries scan vast amounts of data and can severely degrade the performance of these operational databases, organizations replicate their data into a separate, read-only **data warehouse**. 

Through an [[ETL]] process, data from disparate source systems is pulled, cleaned, transformed into an analysis-friendly format, and loaded into this central repository without impacting live transactions. While smaller businesses can often perform analysis directly on operational databases, large-scale analytics demands this separation not just for workload isolation, but because OLTP indexing strategies (like B-trees and LSM-trees) perform poorly on broad analytic scans.

Consequently, database architectures have diverged: despite presenting similar SQL interfaces on the surface, transactional systems and analytical warehouses rely on radically different internal storage engines. This divergence has driven the market toward specialized analytical databases, ranging from commercial systems like Snowflake, Vertica, and Amazon Redshift to open-source distributed engines like Presto, Trino, and Spark SQL.

In a typical data warehouse, tables are often very wide: fact tables often have over 100 columns, sometimes several hundred. Dimension tables can also be very wide, as they include all the metadata that may be relevant for analysis—for example, the dim_store table may include details of which services are offered at each store, whether it has an in-store bakery, the square footage, the date when the store was first opened, when it was last remodeled, how far it is from the nearest highway, etc.
